## 2) Synthesis: what practitioners believe today

1. **CEX-CEX arbitrage is not “click-transfer-sell” anymore; it is an inventory and latency problem.** The clearest opened practitioner statement is CCXT’s: transfers take minutes to hours, so the working version is pre-positioned inventory on both venues, simultaneous execution, and later rebalancing. This maps directly to P6’s latency-aware EV framing: transfer time should not be in the critical path, but rebalancing drag belongs in fees/costs.

2. **The meaningful latency budget is venue- and region-specific, often tens to hundreds of ms before strategy code runs.** AWS/Avelacom’s measured Tokyo cross-region RTTs show that Tokyo→Singapore was already ~65–68 ms round trip, while Tokyo→EU/US was ~124–245 ms depending on route and network. Glassnode’s thesis is the same: wrong-city deployment loses by tens to hundreds of ms.

3. **Practitioners measure both market-data latency and execution/order latency; backtests that ignore either are suspect.** hftbacktest and NautilusTrader both emphasize feed timing, order latency, queue/fill simulation, multi-venue replay, and research/live parity. Tardis preserves WebSocket glitches, publish delays and local timestamps instead of cleaning data into idealized REST history.

4. **Clock/timestamp work is a first-class problem.** Tardis exposes both exchange timestamps and local receive timestamps. That enables signal-decay and stale-quote studies, but it also warns that exchange timestamp reliability and feed-specific batching/latency must be measured rather than assumed.

5. **On CEX-DEX, latency is real but the market structure is different.** Hyperliquid docs explicitly say end-to-end latency is not apples-to-apples with centralized matching because API, mempool and consensus commit are part of the path; maker-protective ordering means ALO/cancels can precede IOC/GTC flow sent at the same time. Ethereum CEX-DEX evidence shows searcher-builder integration and concentration dominate profits.

6. **Funding/basis trades are less latency-critical but still cost/risk-critical.** CCXT calls funding-rate arb an institutional favorite because it scales and does not need speed, but funding can flip, derivatives margin can liquidate, and entry/exit costs include two spreads. This is a good “wait / slower lane” contrast for P6’s act-now vs wait framework.

7. **Optiver-style judging likely rewards microstructure realism and validation discipline, not just a threshold detector.** Opened Optiver/Kaggle pages did not render enough to cite winning solutions, but the community Optiver volatility repo and hftbacktest docs converge on features and validation themes: WAP/log returns/realized vol, spreads/imbalances, subinterval windows, robust CV, and realistic replay/fill assumptions.

## 3) Contradictions / controversies

- **“Arb is dead” vs “arb exists but is professionalized.”** Opened sources did not provide a primary X/Reddit claim that CEX-CEX is dead. The best opened practitioner counter-frame is CCXT: cross-exchange inventory arb remains a classic use case, but the edge is latency, sizing, fee tier, depth and venue selection rather than the idea.
- **CEX speed vs on-chain transparency/fairness.** Hyperliquid positions itself differently from CEXs: on-chain sequencing and maker protection create predictable ordering but not centralized-engine latency. A P6 demo should not rank CEX and on-chain venues with one undifferentiated latency model.
- **Shorter block time does not automatically mean less LP loss or more arb profit.** The Ethereum Research post argues gas/basefee, swap fees and liquidity depth can dominate block-time effects, contradicting simplistic “faster chain = less arbitrage loss” narratives.
- **Marketing latency claims vs measured latency.** RisingWave says opportunities vanish in milliseconds/hundreds of ms, while AWS gives actual route RTTs and Tardis/Glassnode focus on measurement. Treat generic “milliseconds” claims as hypotheses until measured on the selected venues/region.
- **Backtest optimism is controversial.** hftbacktest explicitly warns that small edges require accurate live-like backtests. NautilusTrader cautions that live introduces transport/timing/reconciliation behaviors simulation may not reproduce. P6 should make this limitation visible.

## 4) Implications for P6

What would likely impress Optiver/IIT Bombay judges:

1. **Rank opportunities by net, latency-aware EV, not raw spread.** Use: expected edge after taker/maker fees, slippage/depth, inventory constraints, rebalancing drag, and `P(alive after latency)`.
2. **Show empirical decay curves.** For each venue pair or spread family, estimate survival/half-life from recorded BBO/order-book data using local receive timestamps; report median/P90 decay and sensitivity to extra 10/50/100 ms.
3. **Separate strategy lanes.** CEX-CEX stale-quote sniping, spot-perp/funding basis, correlated-asset spreads, and CEX-DEX/on-chain opportunities have different latency clocks and cost models.
4. **Model geography.** Include region assumptions: India Mac recording vs AWS Tokyo/Singapore vs US/EU. AWS route numbers make a strong concrete benchmark for why location matters.
5. **Use Tardis/live WebSocket realism.** Preserve exchange vs local timestamps, dropped/delayed messages, and different update rates. Do not resample everything into clean candles before measuring opportunity decay.
6. **Add an inventory pre-positioning dashboard.** Display whether both legs are executable now given balances, venue status, min sizes, and post-trade inventory imbalance.
7. **Paper-trading demo should account for fill uncertainty.** hftbacktest/Nautilus-style queue/fill/order-latency assumptions are more impressive than a “signal fired” log.
8. **Trap to avoid: ticker-only spreads.** CCXT says depth before tickers. Ranking by last price or top-of-book without size, fees and latency will look naïve.
