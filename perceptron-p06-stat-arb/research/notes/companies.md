## Optiver (host of this track's starter context; closest primary source to the problem statement)

- **Kaggle "Trading at the Close"**: task is to predict the 60-second-forward WAP return of a stock *relative to a synthetic index* of all competition stocks, from an order-book + closing-auction-imbalance snapshot during the last 10 minutes of the Nasdaq trading day; scored on MAE. Source: kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close (overview/data pages, opened via search-cache; direct fetch returned only the page title due to JS rendering — treat task description as **lightly verified**, re-confirm by opening the Kaggle "Data" tab directly before citing in the abstract).
- **"Designing for latency and research iteration"** (Optiver technology blog, by Daniel B., engineering team lead, Optiver Shanghai; published 20 Jul 2026). Key claims, quoted/paraphrased directly from the article body (extracted from the page's embedded JSON, since the rendered HTML is JS-only):
  - Optiver's stack went through **three generations**: (1) traders composing strategies as large visual graphs — fast to prototype, but "graphs grow into forests... running all of it on the live path gets too heavy. Latency was not competitive by later standards"; (2) a compiled approach — strategy logic in a higher-level language compiled to native code on a thin runtime, which "unlocked machine learning in research and production, and pushed latency into a much faster band," but the compiler itself became the new bottleneck and organizational friction point; (3) current direction — **native code end-to-end, collapsing the layers between market data arriving and orders going out** ("tick-to-trade"), aiming for rich research abstractions at "close to zero run-time cost."
  - Explicit design goals stated: "meaningfully lower end-to-end latency, latency that does not degrade as features and models accumulate, and smoother promotion from research into production."
  - Direct relevance to P6: Optiver frames the central engineering trade-off as *latency vs. research-iteration speed*, and stresses keeping "simulation honest relative to production" so that backtested edge is reproducible live — this is exactly the signal-confidence-vs-acting-latency trade-off our problem statement asks teams to model.
  - URL: https://www.optiver.com/insights/technology-blog/designing-for-latency-and-iteration/ (dateTime 2026-07-20).

Talk covers: "key principles for designing low latency trading systems," "using concurrent data structures to reduce latency," "techniques for optimizing algorithms and data structures." ## Data infrastructure for multi-exchange crypto feeds

- **Tardis.dev** (tardis.dev): ships historical tick-level datasets — L2 incremental book updates, L2/L3 snapshots, trades, quotes, options chains, funding rates, liquidations — "updated daily," with explicit schema columns `timestamp` (exchange-reported, microseconds) vs. `local_timestamp` (Tardis receipt time, microseconds) side by side in the sample table, e.g. a Deribit BTC-PERPETUAL snapshot row showing `timestamp=1585699209920000`, `local_timestamp=1585699209934201` — i.e., **~14ms exchange-to-collector latency** visible directly in the sample row. Direct relevance to P6: this timestamp pair is exactly the kind of "signal arrival vs. actionable arrival" gap our latency-budget model needs, and Tardis publishes it natively per-record rather than requiring us to estimate it. ## Execution / backtesting / bot frameworks

- **hummingbot/hummingbot** (GitHub): open-source "framework that helps you design and deploy automated trading strategies... that can run on many centralized or decentralized exchanges." Stated usage: "Over the past year, Hummingbot users have generated over **$34 billion** in trading volume across **140+ unique trading venues**." Architecture has split into Condor (AI/agentic harness) → Hummingbot API (server) → Hummingbot Client + Gateway (execution/DEX middleware). Real-time aggregate-volume dashboard at reporting.hummingbot.org. URLs: github.com/hummingbot/hummingbot; hummingbot.org (homepage, opened directly — also carries user testimonials from market-making firms CoinAlpha, 3Jane, Enflux describing Hummingbot's WebSocket connector architecture as a production foundation for their market-making businesses).
- **hftbacktest** (nkaz001/hftbacktest, GitHub): "framework designed for developing high frequency trading and market making strategies," explicitly modeling **both feed and order latency** plus **order-queue position** for fill simulation; full L2 (MBP) and L3 (MBO) order book reconstruction; JIT via Numba for Python, with a Rust core (Rust 1.90+, crates.io); supports multi-asset/multi-exchange backtests and can deploy the *same* strategy code live on Binance Futures and Bybit (Rust-only live path). Explicit design philosophy quoted from the README: backtesting must avoid being either "overly pessimistic... [hiding] small edges" or "overly optimistic... [overstating] them through unrealistic simulation," and if you run the same strategy live vs. backtested over the same period, "the backtest for that exact period should produce results that closely align with the actual results" — directly actionable as P6's evaluation-plan design (latency-injected replay vs. live PnL parity check). URL: https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest.
- **NautilusTrader** (nautilustrader.io): "production-grade, Rust-native trading engine infrastructure," marketed around "deterministic simulation," "live execution," and "research-to-live parity" (page `<meta>`/keywords, opened directly). Relevant to P6 as a reference architecture for keeping backtest and live code paths identical — directly addresses the brief's ask for "an event-driven backtester with injected latency." URL: https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/. Gap: full docs body is behind client-side JS rendering; could not extract specific latency numbers or benchmark figures from this pass — **UNVERIFIED pending a JS-capable fetch of a specific docs subpage (e.g., a "performance" or "benchmarks" page).**
- **cryptofeed** (bmoscon/cryptofeed, GitHub): Python feed handler normalizing multiple exchange WS feeds into common callback objects for TRADES, L1/L2/L3_BOOK, TICKER, FUNDING, OPEN_INTEREST, LIQUIDATIONS, INDEX, CANDLES; ships a **synthetic NBBO (National Best Bid/Offer)** aggregator across user-specified exchanges (`add_nbbo([Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini], ...)`) — i.e., ready-made cross-venue best-quote computation, directly reusable for P6's cross-exchange mispricing detector. Also supports pcap recording/replay (zstd-compressed) for deterministic backtests. Requires Python 3.13+. URL: https://github.com/bmoscon/cryptofeed.
- **CCXT / CCXT Pro**: "100+ exchanges, one unified API" across crypto and prediction-market venues, with WS streaming ("Pro") in JS/Python/PHP/C#/Go/Java/Rust — the standard off-the-shelf multi-exchange connectivity layer teams reach for before building bespoke feed handlers. URL: https://docs.ccxt.com/#/pro.

## MEV / on-chain latency infra (cross-venue arb at the blockchain layer)

This is the primary venue-level analogue of "latency budget vs. signal decay" in DeFi: arbers compete for block inclusion rather than wire speed. ## P6 implications (synthesis, not a new citation)

1. **Timestamp-pair data already exists**: Tardis's own sample schema (`timestamp` vs `local_timestamp`) means a credible "signal decay" baseline can be built without inventing latency — pull real exchange-vs-receipt deltas per venue instead of only injecting synthetic latency.
2. **cryptofeed's NBBO aggregator** is a near-direct scaffold for the cross-exchange mispricing detector in the Minimum Viable bullet — worth citing as "don't reinvent"; our innovation should be in the statistical/ranking model and the latency-vs-confidence decision rule, not the feed plumbing.
3. **hftbacktest's "backtest-production parity" framing** (and NautilusTrader's identical "research-to-live parity" pitch) gives a direct evaluation-plan template: show that an event-driven backtester with injected feed/order latency reproduces the actual decay rate observed in real tick data, not just a toy Monte Carlo.
4. **Optiver's own stated trade-off (latency vs. iteration speed)** is a strong frame for the abstract's "core technical difficulty in our own words": propose that confidence and latency are jointly optimized, mirroring Optiver's three-generation story of trading expressiveness against pure speed.
5. **Hyperliquid's fee tiers + maker rebate** give concrete numbers to parameterize the "expected value of acting now vs. waiting, given latency, fees and confidence" decision rule from the brief — e.g., a mispricing below ~2×(taker+taker) round-trip fee is not worth acting on at base tier.
6. **On-chain arb (Flashbots/Hyperliquid) is a legitimate second domain** for "cross-venue" beyond CEX-vs-CEX crypto: block-inclusion latency and private-mempool auctions are a structurally different (slower, discrete) latency regime than exchange websockets, useful as a stretch-goal comparison.
