Introduction & Vision

The IITB-OPtiver AI Innovation Lab Hackathon invites builders to tackle ten
concrete, technically demanding problems spanning efficient AI systems, causal
reasoning, financial markets, multi-agent coordination, education, and trustworthy
AI. Unlike a single open-ended brief, each theme below bundles multiple related
problem statements. Teams pick one theme, then one specific problem within it,
and are evaluated on the depth and rigor of their technical plan, not a working
prototype, at this stage.


Competition Themes
Theme 1: Efficient AI at Scale

Problem 1: Cheap Fine-Tuning Across Weak, Scattered Devices

Context:Organizations often want to specialize a shared model using data that lives
         on many different machines without moving the raw data anywhere.
Challenge:Build a lightweight fine-tuning scheme where even low-power devices
           can meaningfully contribute updates to a shared model, training stays
           stable even when devices drop out mid-run, and one data-rich device
           can't quietly dominate the result.
Starter Resources:HuggingFace PEFT (LoRA) + an open federated learning
                  framework (e.g. Flower), applied to a small open model (GPT-2 or
                 TinyLlama scale)
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Simulate 3–5 nodes with different data splits and compute budgets
   ●​ Show the shared model converges despite the heterogeneity
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Simulate node dropout mid-training
   ●​ Compare a smarter aggregation strategy against naive averaging

Problem 2: Squeezing Long-Context Inference into Less Memory

Context: Long documents or chat histories strain GPU memory, mostly from the
         cache a model keeps for past tokens.
Challenge: Shrink that cache significantly while barely hurting the model's ability to
           recall earlier content.



Starter Resources: Open reference implementations of existing cache-compression
                  techniques (e.g. StreamingLLM, H2O), applied to a small model
                  (1-3B params)
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Apply an existing compression technique and measure memory savings vs.
      an uncompressed baseline
   ●​ Show the accuracy drop on a small, long-context recall task
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Propose a modification to an existing method
   ●​ Benchmark against 2+ baselines

Note: Choose either Problem 1 or Problem 2 (not both) as your team's single
problem statement for Round 1.

Theme 2: Cause and Effect in AI Decisions

Problem 3: Did the Policy Actually Work, or Did It Just Look Like It Did?

Context: A deployed decision system shapes the very outcomes later used to judge
         it,so simple before/after metrics can mislead.
Challenge: Estimate what would have happened under a different policy, using only
            logged data from the current one.
Starter Resources: A public bandit-feedback or off-policy evaluation dataset (e.g.
                    Open Bandit Dataset), or a synthetic dataset with known
                    ground-truth counterfactuals.
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Apply a standard off-policy evaluation method (e.g. inverse propensity
      weighting or a doubly robust estimator)
   ●​ Show a concrete case where this estimate differs from a naive before/after
      number
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Quantify uncertainty on the counterfactual estimate
   ●​ A basic sensitivity analysis for unobserved confounding

Note: This theme currently has a single problem statement.


Theme 3: Markets & Trading Intelligence

Problem 4: Order Book Forecasting Under Regime Shifts

Context:Limit order book dynamics change abruptly around macro announcements,
         volatility spikes, and liquidity shocks. Models trained on one regime often
         degrade silently when the regime changes.
Challenge: Build a short-horizon LOB forecasting model that detects when the data
           distribution has shifted and adapts, instead of silently degrading.
           Starter Resources: Public LOB datasets (e.g. LOBSTER sample data, or
           crypto exchange order-book data via exchange APIs)
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Predict short-term price direction or spread from order book features
   ●​ Detect at least one distribution shift using a simple statistical test (e.g. rolling
      KS test) and show the model reacting to it
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Multiple regime-adaptive prediction heads
   ●​ Latency benchmarking under a defined budget

Problem 5: Market-Impact-Aware Execution Strategy

Context: A firm's own large orders move the market it's trying to trade in, so naive
         models overstate expected returns.
Challenge: Build an execution agent that reduces slippage compared to a standard
          baseline by accounting for its own market impact.
Starter Resources: An open market simulator (e.g. ABIDES)
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Implement a TWAP/VWAP baseline
   ●​ Build one agent (rule-based, heuristic, or simple RL) that measurably beats
      the baseline on slippage in the simulator
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Explicit counterfactual modeling of "what the book would look like without
      our order"
   ●​ Multi-step feedback-loop analysis

Problem 6: Cross-Asset Statistical Arbitrage with Latency Budgets
Context: Arbitrage opportunities across correlated instruments exist for only
         milliseconds before they close.
Challenge:Identify and rank cross-asset mispricings in near real time, trading off
           Signal confidence against acting-on-it latency.
Starter Resources: Synthetic paired price series, or public multi-exchange crypto
                    price data for the same asset.

Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Detect mispricings using a threshold or statistical model
   ●​ Report how fast the opportunity typically decays
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Simulated end-to-end latency budget
   ●​ A basic paper-trading demo
Note: Choose exactly one of Problems 4, 5, or 6 as your team's problem statement.


Theme 4: Agents That Compete, Cooperate, and Adapt

Problem 7: Splitting a Shared Resource When Demand Keeps Changing

Context: Delivery fleets, compute clusters, or robot swarms must divide limited
         resources among agents while demand shifts unpredictably.
Challenge: Build a multi-agent system that reallocates resources as demand shifts,
           using some form of look-ahead rather than a fixed static policy.
Starter Resources: An open multi-agent simulation environment (e.g. a grid-world
                   dispatch simulator, or PettingZoo-based environment) provided
                   or pointed to by organizers.
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Implement a rule-based baseline allocator
   ●​ Build one agent (RL or planning-based) that outperforms it when demand
      suddenly shifts
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Handle both an abrupt shock and a gradual drift in the same run
   ●​ Evaluate over long horizons, not just short episodes

Note: This theme currently has a single problem statement.




Theme 5: AI That Teaches

Problem 8: A Tutor That Finds the Real Reason You're Stuck

Context: Struggling with quant reasoning, math, or code usually isn't about missing
         facts it's one specific misunderstanding producing the same class of
         mistake repeatedly.
Challenge: Build a tutoring system that traces a learner's wrong answers back to a
            likely root misunderstanding and generates targeted follow-up problems.
Starter Resources: A public dataset of student errors (e.g. ASSISTments, or a
coding-error dataset), or a small hand-built taxonomy of 3–5 common
misconceptions if no dataset fits.

Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Classify a wrong answer into one of 3–5 misconception categories
   ●​ Automatically generate a new practice problem targeted at that category
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Track a learner's misconceptions across multiple sessions
   ●​ Show some evidence the targeted problems actually reduce that error type
      over time

Note: This theme currently has a single problem statement.


Theme 6: AI You Can Actually Trust

Problem 9: Catching an Agent Before It Acts on a Bad Idea

Context: Agents that call tools and take multi-step actions can hallucinate a
plausible but ungrounded action.
Challenge: Build a guardrail layer that checks a proposed action against a verifiable
source before it executes.
Starter Resources: An existing agent framework (LangChain/LangGraph or similar)
plus a small reference knowledge base for grounding checks.
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Build a checker that verifies at least one class of agent action against a
       source before execution
   ●​ Demonstrate it catching at least one deliberately bad/hallucinated action
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Add routing between a fast/cheap model and a slower/careful one based on
       risk
   ●​ A small red-teaming test suite



Problem 10: Trusting a Signal Extracted from Messy, Manipulated Text

Context: Decisions increasingly lean on signals from unstructured text alongside
numeric data, but that text is noisy and sometimes manipulated.
Challenge: Fuse text and time-series data into one signal and report a confidence
bound on each prediction that's meaningful.
Starter Resources: A public financial news + price dataset (widely available on
Kaggle),plus an existing conformal prediction library (e.g. MAPIE) instead of
building calibration machinery from scratch.
Minimum Viable:
   ●​ Build a fusion model combining text sentiment and time-series signal
   ●​ Wrap predictions with an off-the-shelf conformal prediction method for
       confidence bounds
Stretch Goals:
   ●​ Test robustness under one adversarial or noisy-label scenario
   ●​ Check whether the confidence bounds are actually well-calibrated, not just
       present

Note: Choose either Problem 9 or Problem 10 as your team's problem statement.


Competition Structure & Timeline


Eligibility & Team Rules

   ● Team Size: A maximum of 3 members per team.
   ● Eligibility: Open to all students with a valid ID from their educational
     institution. Cross-institutional teams are encouraged.


Round Structure

Round 1: Online abstract submission, evaluated by a panel against the
         criteria below.

Round 2: Finals. A total of 25-30 teams are selected across all themes combined
(not 30 per theme) to advance to the in-person finals at IIT Bombay.Teams must
bring a working code / prototype demonstrating their Minimum Viable scope (and
any stretch goals attempted).Round 1 was plan-only, but Round 2 requires an
actual functioning build to present and demo to the judges.

Midway Check : Roughly 15 days before the finals, each of these shortlisted teams
submits a 1-page progress report. This is a lightweight checkpoint to confirm the
team is on track and has not stalled or pivoted away from its stated plan . it is not
separately scored, but flags teams needing support or follow-up before the final
judging.



Finals : At the finals, each team presents to a joint panel of judges from Optiver
and IIT Bombay professors. Scores from the panel are normalized across all 30
teams (regardless of theme) on a common scale, and the top 3 teams overall (not
top 3 per theme) are declared the winners.



Teams choose one problem statement from the 10 listed across the 6 themes
(Efficient AI at Scale, Cause & Effect in AI Decisions, Markets & Trading Intelligence,
Agents, AI That Teaches, AI You Can Trust).
