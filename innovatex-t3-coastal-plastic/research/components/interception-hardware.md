## TL;DR
1. **Mumbai already runs nullah trash booms.** BMC: 9 steel-net trash booms with conveyors since ~2022, 14 more planned (network → 23), "Each unit costs approximately ₹ 1 crore"; each "collects about 1.5 metric tonnes of garbage per day" dry season, "2.5–3 metric tonnes during the monsoon" *(municipal figures reported by HT)*. A ₹14.5-crore, two-year O&M contract covers booms on six rivers/nullahs in the western suburbs (FPJ, 30 Jul 2026). → The baseline we must beat is **not "no barrier"**; it is *un-instrumented* barriers with daily manual clearing and no verified kg.
2. **The Ocean Cleanup is deploying Interceptors at Trombay and Malad creeks in 2026**, expecting "61 to 92 tonnes of plastic per year" for the initial systems *(claim/forecast)*; ops by Plastic Fischer (see companies.md).
3. **Booms fail by physics above ~0.35 m/s normal current.** ITOPF (oil-spill booms, the best-documented analogue): "In practice, the escape velocity for most booms is around 0.35 ms-1 (0.7 knots) irrespective of skirt depth"; booms are "rarely capable of containing oil against water velocities much in excess of 0.5 ms-1 (1 knot) acting at right angles to it"; at "1.5 ms-1 current (3 knots) ... a boom positioned at right angles to the flow would fail to contain any oil". Failure modes: entrainment, drainage failure, critical accumulation, splash-over, submergence, planing. → **Angle the boom** so the normal velocity component stays below the escape velocity; plastic films behave more like entrained oil than like bottles (inference).
4. **Bubble barriers** are boat- and flow-transparent but rely on a deflecting current: Amsterdam's 60 m curtain caught "some 190,000 pieces of plastic waste a year, weighing around one tonne" (city report via DutchNews); 86% is the city's *expected* capture, not an independent measurement in that article; five more cost "€600,000, plus €50,000 a year". ITOPF notes bubble curtains hold oil only "against a water flow of up to 0.35 ms-1".
5. **Trash wheel**: Mr. Trash Wheel "uses a unique blend of solar and hydro power to collect 500 tons of litter and debris each year" *(operator claim)* — a fixed civil structure at one outfall.
6. **Interceptor Original** (TOC): "100% solar-powered", barrier guides waste to a catamaran; "conveyor belt ... delivers the waste to the shuttle"; "distributes the debris across six dumpsters. Using sensor data, the containers are filled equally"; "When ... almost full, it automatically sends a text message to the local operators" *(claim)* → **fill sensing + operator alerting already exists inside the best system**; our novelty must be forecast/tide-aware *dispatch across many cheap sites* and *verified kg*, not "a sensor that texts".
7. **Mop-up robots are complements**: WasteShark+ "160-litre basket", "up to 5,000 m² (1.2 acres) daily"; Clearbot "up to 500 kg in a single deployment" *(claims)* — too small for monsoon pulses at a nullah mouth.
8. **Flood risk**: a net/screen across a drain adds head loss and, when blinded by debris in a cloudburst, can back water up into streets; screens need a bypass/overflow and clearance before peaks — see §5 (UK EA screen guide status below).

## 2. Failure modes (oil-boom analogue, ITOPF Technical Information Paper 3)
Source: ITOPF, "Use of booms in oil pollution response" (TIP 3), PDF https://www.itopf.org/fileadmin/uploads/itopf/data/Documents/TIPS_TAPS_new/TIP_3_Use_of_Booms_in_Oil_Pollution_Response.pdf
- Escape velocity: "In practice, the escape velocity for most booms is around 0.35 ms-1 (0.7 knots) irrespective of skirt depth"; containment "against water velocities much in excess of 0.5 ms-1 (1 knot) acting at right angles to it" is rare.
- Angling: Table 2 gives "Maximum deployment angles to flow direction at different current strengths ... Calculations are based on an escape velocity of 0.7 knots"; even at "1.5 ms-1 current (3 knots)" a boom at right angles "would fail to contain any oil" while an angled boom can still deflect.
- Named failure modes (Figure 3): **entrainment, drainage failure, critical accumulation, splash-over, submergence, planing**.
- Bubble curtains: "The air bubbles create a countercurrent on the surface that holds the oil against a water flow of up to 0.35 ms-1".
- **Translation to plastic (inference, to test in the flume):** normal-velocity component `u_n = U·sin(θ)` must stay below an escape velocity for films/sachets; θ = boom angle to flow. For U = 1.0 m/s monsoon outflow and u_esc = 0.35 m/s → θ ≤ asin(0.35) ≈ 20°. This is the core design rule for a Mumbai nullah boom and a measurable flume experiment (capture % vs U and θ).
- Peer-reviewed flume studies of plastic-specific booms found via search (MDPI Water 16(2):248 "Removing Plastic Waste from Rivers: A Prototype-Scale..." and IAHR 2022 floating debris barrier papers) could not be opened (HTTP 403 / not fetched) → **UNVERIFIED**; do not cite numbers from them yet.

## 3. Flood and tide handling — what the opened sources say, and gaps
- No opened source gives flood or tide behaviour for BMC booms, Interceptors or TrashBooms → **NOT FOUND**. This is a genuine gap we can own: Mumbai outfalls are tidal (flow reverses on the flood tide) and monsoon peaks coincide with high tides.
- Design implications (ours, to validate): (1) boom anchored with slack/sliding moorings on piles so it rises with tide and flood stage; (2) skirt shallow + angled so overtopping/planing releases load before structural failure; (3) a **fuse/bypass**: the boom is designed to release or swing open above a stage/velocity threshold so it never becomes a dam (flood-safety requirement); (4) clear before the forecast peak and at low water — this is where tide- and rain-forecast-aware dispatch pays.

## 5. Head loss and flood risk of screens and nets in drains
- UK Environment Agency "Trash and security screen guide" (2009) is the standard reference on screen blockage and head loss; the GOV.UK publication URL tried on 2026-10-04 (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trash-and-security-screen-guide) returned 404, so the guide was **NOT opened**. Without it, quantitative head-loss coefficients are **UNVERIFIED** here.
- Qualitative rule (inference): a net that spans the full section turns into a weir when blinded; a floating boom with a shallow skirt does not reduce flood conveyance below the skirt, which is why booms (not full-depth nets) are the right baseline for flood-prone nullahs — and why we should never propose full-depth nets at outfalls without an overflow path.
